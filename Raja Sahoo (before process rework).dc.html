I design products, not screens.

A screen is what's left after the hard part. The hard part is deciding what the product is, what it refuses to do, and which of the forty things a user could see actually earns the space.

Most of my work is on products where the difficulty is invisible — timetables, inventory, prescriptions, guest lists. So I hold the strategy and the pixels together: on small teams, the seam between them is exactly where products go wrong.

Context

Every Friday plan dies in a queue.

Finding somewhere nearby is solved — maps do it fine. What isn't solved is the forty minutes after you arrive: the table that isn't free, the lane that's booked, the salon chair with four people ahead of you.

Wisbox puts discovery and commitment on the same screen. You browse what's around you — food, games, places to hang out, services — and pre-book the slot, the table or the product before you've put your shoes on.

At a glance

Context

The tutor's world is small, and completely full.

Six batches a day. Forty to two hundred students. Fees arriving as cash, UPI screenshots and promises. Attendance in a notebook that lives in a bag. Parents who want to know how their child is doing — on WhatsApp, at ten at night.

None of it is difficult. All of it is constant. The actual job is the ninety minutes at a whiteboard; everything around it was taking about two hours a day.

The alternatives were school ERPs built for institutions with an admin department, or single-purpose apps that solved a tenth of the job and left the tutor to be the integration layer between them.

Who it's for

One operator, one recipient, and a record that isn't a user.

Getting this wrong is how tuition software ends up with three logins. Only the tutor operates the product; the parent receives from it; the student is data the product carries. Deciding that early removed a whole app from the roadmap.

The key insight

The tutor isn't entering data. The tutor is reporting exceptions.

Watch anyone take attendance on paper and they don't tick forty names — they scan the room, spot the empty chairs, and write down two. The notebook is a record of what went wrong. Every attendance UI I looked at had inverted that: forty decisions, one per student, every single day.

Decision one

Attendance defaults to present.

Open a batch and the register is already complete. Everyone is present. The tutor marks the two who aren't, and closes the session. A full batch — the most common outcome by a wide margin — costs zero taps.

The trade-off is real: a distracted tutor could close without looking. So the close button carries the count, marking someone absent is a one-tap undo, and a session left untouched past its end time nudges once — then stays untouched rather than guessing.

one less decision, forty times a day →

Station 01 · problem framing

Four frictions, all of them weekly, none of them priced by anyone.

Pain 01 · fee leakage

Fees live in a diary or in memory. Some parents forget, some pay late, some short-pay, and there is no clean defaulter list.

5–15% of billings, every month

Pain 02 · awkward chasing

Asking a parent for money face to face costs the relationship, so she delays — which makes the leakage worse. There is no polite, automatic, deniable channel.

The delay is the design problem

Pain 03 · zero parent visibility

A parent pays ₹1,000–3,000 a month and receives nothing back. No attendance, no marks, no report.

Trust rests on an exam result months away

Pain 04 · everything manual

Attendance, scheduling, marks, receipts, reminders — all by hand. Her scarcest resource is spent on paperwork instead of teaching.

Admin is the competitor

The real incumbent is not another app. It is a paper diary, a WhatsApp thread and a spreadsheet — free, familiar, and leaking. Anything that replaces it has to be lighter than paper, not richer than software.

Station 03 · journey

The same evening, twice.

Nothing is added to her evening. The four grey blocks are replaced by four taps and a background job — which is why the pitch is “reports are generated, never authored”.

Station 04 · information architecture

Five surfaces in the app. WhatsApp is the sixth, and it belongs to somebody else.

This is the decision the whole product hangs from. The tutor is the only account. The parent is reached on a surface we do not own, cannot design, and must therefore treat as a first-class output of the system rather than a notification channel.

What this buys

Every parent is already installed. The funnel loses nobody to a download, a login or a password reset.

What it costs

Every message is a metered template with a category price, so the message plan is the unit economics. Design has to be frugal by nature.

What it forbids

No screen can assume a parent will “open the app and see”. If it matters, it has to survive as one WhatsApp message.

Branch — mandate fails

Premium lapses at cycle end, not instantly. The child drops to the free tier and the parent keeps the absence alerts — the trust that sold the subscription is never withdrawn as a punishment.

Branch — no signal

Attendance is captured offline and reconciled on the session key. A tutor in a basement classroom still closes her register; the outbound message queues behind her.

Branch — edited later

Corrections are allowed inside the open cycle and the report recomputes from live data. A month-end PDF is a render of the truth, never a snapshot that can drift from it.

Decision two — timetable

The timetable is the home screen, because the day is the unit of work.

Not a dashboard of totals. The tutor opens the app between classes, so the first thing on screen is the next class, the students in it, and the one action it needs. Everything else is reachable, and nothing else is presented.

Decision three

Money is a state, not a report.

There is no accounting section to visit. Every student carries their due state with them — in the batch list, in attendance, in search — because the moment a tutor thinks about fees is the moment they're looking at the student, not a ledger. Collecting is two taps from wherever that happens.

Decision four

Parents never get an app.

A parent portal would have been the obvious decision and the wrong one. These parents already have a channel they check, and adding a second guarantees the tutor gets asked the same question twice.

So the parent surface is WhatsApp: an absence the same evening, the monthly report as a document, a fee reminder that doesn't read like a collections notice. Templated, so nobody composes a message at 10pm.

Designing inside someone else's UI means giving up layout. What's left is timing, tone and length — which turned out to be the whole job.

At a glance

Inventory isn't a counting problem. It's a forecasting problem wearing a spreadsheet.

A kitchen already knows its stock — it's in someone's head, a voice note and a notebook near the walk-in. The problem isn't recording it. It's that by the time anyone counts, the decision that needed the count has already been made badly.

So usage is derived from orders. Stock moves as service happens. What the kitchen gets isn't a database — it's an answer to the only two questions they actually ask: what runs out first, and what do I order today.

The hardest part was tone. Everything about the category invites a dark dashboard covered in confidence scores. A tool used at 11pm by a tired chef should feel like a well-organised kitchen: calm, legible, nothing blinking.

    Chest pain, breathlessness, a child under two — the intake stops mid-sentence and hands over. It's the one place in the product where the interface is deliberately abrupt, because a smooth transition would suggest the situation is normal.

  • Every machine-written line is marked as one

    The doctor sees a draft summary, visibly attributed and fully editable. Nothing the model wrote reaches the patient without a doctor touching it.

  • Credentials are an object, not a badge

    Verified onboarding puts registration number, council and specialisation on a record the patient can open — because a blue tick is exactly as trustworthy as the platform showing it.

  • A case board for second opinions

    Doctors can put an anonymised case in front of peers. Designed as a quiet clinical board, not a feed — no votes, no reactions, no reply counts.

The address is the last thing you earn.

A private house party has a social structure that public event apps get wrong in the first screen. There's a host with something to lose, guests who need to be vouched for, and a location that cannot be public information.

So privacy is the spine of the flow. You see the neighbourhood, then the area, and the exact address only once you're approved, an hour before. The product's emotional arc is the gap between wanting in and being let in — and the design leans into that tension rather than smoothing it away.

Social proof is who you know, never how many are going. A count turns a living room into a venue.

It never scores your face. That was the product decision, and everything else followed it.

Every AI beauty tool I looked at ends up in the same place: a number, a grade, a list of things wrong with you. It's an easy product to build because the model can do it — and it makes the moment in front of the mirror worse.

Mira starts from the opposite premise. You stand in front of it, and it says something true and kind, once. Tips exist, but you ask for them. Products get recommended only when you've asked what to do about something specific.

Every capability we removed took away a way to feel worse. The restraint is the feature — a mirror with opinions about your face is not a mirror you'd keep.

the AI does less on purpose

  • One affirmation, chosen not generated

    From a written set, reviewed by people. A model improvising compliments about someone's appearance is a risk with no upside.

  • Tips are options, never corrections

    Opt-in, phrased as things you could try. Nothing in the copy implies a before and an after.

  • On-device, and the UI says so

    Nothing leaves the mirror. The privacy claim is architectural, so the interface can state plainly where the model runs — the one piece of technical detail worth showing a user.

Stated in the product definition — not a user interview

Everything in Mira is downstream of that sentence. It's a promise about a feeling, which makes it a design constraint rather than a feature list: the product may reassure, may explain when asked, and may never assess.

How I think

Four rules that survive contact with real projects

  1. 01

    Decide first, design second

    Most interface mess is an unmade product decision wearing a layout. Naming the decision usually deletes half the screen.

  2. 02

    Design the exception

    If 95% of the input is the same, the interface should assume it and only ask about the rest. Vidwan's register is this rule as a product.

  3. 03

    System before screen

    Objects, states, permissions and empties get drawn before anything gets styled. It's cheaper to be wrong in a diagram.

  4. 04

    Earn every animation

    If it doesn't explain hierarchy or continuity, it's noise with easing on it.
